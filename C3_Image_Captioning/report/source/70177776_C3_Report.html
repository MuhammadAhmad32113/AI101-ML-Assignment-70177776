Abstract —
Automated image captioning bridges computer vision and natural language processing by synthesizing descriptive textual narratives from visual inputs. Utilizing the benchmark Flickr8k dataset (8,091 images with 5 reference captions each, totalling 40,455 captions), we enforce strict image-level partitioning (6,000 train / 1,000 validation / 1,091 test) to guarantee zero visual leakage. We implement and evaluate two sequence-to-sequence neural architectures: (i) Model 1, combining a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 visual encoder with a recurrent Merge LSTM decoder, and (ii) Model 2, pairing a frozen InceptionV3 backbone with 200-dimensional pretrained GloVe word embeddings. We benchmark greedy decoding against beam search ($k=3, 5$). Model 1 under greedy decoding achieves corpus BLEU-1 of 0.582 and BLEU-4 of 0.148. Beam search ($k=5$) significantly boosts BLEU-4 to 0.182 (+23.0% relative gain) by finding globally optimal sequence hypotheses; however, it reduces distinct vocabulary count by 34.9% (from 642 to 418 distinct words), accentuating generic caption modes. Model 2 achieves superior results across all metrics (greedy BLEU-4: 0.158; beam $k=5$ BLEU-4: 0.194), benefiting from semantic word priors. Error analysis demonstrates that captioning failures are predominantly driven by object counting deficits, background-dominant action misclassifications, and training set demographic biases.
Keywords: Image Captioning, Flickr8k, ResNet-50, InceptionV3, Merge Architecture, LSTM, Beam Search, BLEU Evaluation.

Image captioning addresses the multi-modal task of translating high-dimensional visual scenes into syntactically coherent and semantically grounded natural language sentences [1]. II. Dataset Exploration & Preprocessing

We utilize the standard Flickr8k corpus, consisting of 8,091 everyday photographs depicting people, animals, and outdoor activities. Each image is paired with exactly 5 crowdsourced descriptions, yielding 40,455 total captions.

Fig. 1. Zipfian word frequency decay and vocabulary reduction following minimum frequency thresholding ($c \ge 5$).

A. Image-Level Partitioning: Standard random train/test splits risk leaking visual features if captions for the same image span both training and evaluation subsets. To prevent data leakage, we partition strictly by image identifier: 6,000 images (30,000 captions, 74.2%) for training, 1,000 images (5,000 captions, 12.4%) for validation, and 1,091 images (5,455 captions, 13.5%) for held-out testing ($ ext{Train} \cap ext{Val} \cap ext{Test} = \emptyset$).

B. Text Preprocessing & Vocabulary: Captions average 11.82 words (median 11.0, maximum 37.0). Text is lowercased, punctuation stripped, and bounded with special tokens <start> and <end>. The raw corpus contains 8,918 unique words with a heavy long-tail distribution (Fig. 1). Applying a minimum frequency threshold of $c \ge 5$ discards 6,370 rare hapax legomena while retaining 2,548 words. Including 4 special tokens (<pad>, <start>, <end>, <unk>) establishes a compact, highly reliable vocabulary of 2,552 tokens, maintaining 96.8% token coverage.

III. Methodology & Model Architectures

Following the landmark decoupled multimodal design of Tanti et al. [3], we implement the Merge architecture, which processes visual and textual modalities through independent pathways before merging them at the final feed-forward prediction head.

A. Model 1: ResNet-50 + Merge LSTM

Visual features are extracted from the penultimate average pooling layer of a frozen ImageNet-pretrained ResNet-50 network, yielding a 2048-dimensional dense representation. The Merge model comprises two parallel streams: (i) the image pathway projects the 2048-d vector into a 256-d latent space via a linear layer, ReLU activation, and dropout (0.5); (ii) the text pathway embeds previous word tokens into 256-d vectors and passes them through a 1-layer LSTM (hidden size 256, dropout 0.5). The image representation and LSTM output are concatenated into a 512-d multimodal vector, passed through a 256-d dense layer, and projected onto the 2,552 vocabulary logits. The model contains 3.41M trainable parameters, trained using Adam ($\eta=3 imes 10^{-4}$), cross-entropy loss, and gradient clipping (5.0).

B. Model 2: InceptionV3 + GloVe Pretrained Embeddings

Model 2 introduces two structural variations: (i) an ImageNet-pretrained InceptionV3 CNN backbone providing 2048-d features; (ii) word token embeddings initialized with 200-dimensional pretrained GloVe vectors (glove.6B.200d) [4]. Pretrained vectors provide continuous semantic priors covering 94.9% (2,418/2,548) of the vocabulary, leaving unrepresented tokens randomly initialized ($\sigma=0.01$). Model 2 contains 3.36M trainable parameters.

V. Technical Analysis & Discussion

A. Beam Search: BLEU Gains vs Vocabulary Collapse

Empirical results confirm that expanding the beam width consistently improves corpus BLEU across all orders. For Model 1, beam search ($k=5$) elevates BLEU-4 from 0.148 to 0.182 (+23.0% relative gain) over greedy decoding. However, tracking unique vocabulary utilization exposes an acute trade-off: greedy decoding uses 642 distinct words, whereas beam search ($k=5$) collapses distinct vocabulary usage by 34.9% down to 418 words (Table III). Because beam search optimizes cumulative sequence probability, it penalizes low-frequency, descriptive adjectives and verbs in favor of high-frequency, safe template tokens (e.g., "a dog runs through the grass", "a man is standing on a street"). Consequently, while beam search achieves higher n-gram overlap with crowdsourced references, it generates perceptually homogenized and generic narratives.

B. Pretrained GloVe & Backbone Contributions

Model 2 consistently outperforms Model 1 across all decoding settings (+0.012 BLEU-4 under beam $k=5$). Initializing the embedding layer with GloVe vectors anchors semantically related concepts in latent space (e.g., clustering dog, puppy, and hound), preventing gradient instability during early training epochs. The InceptionV3 feature extractor also demonstrates slightly sharper multi-scale spatial representations than standard ResNet-50 pooling.

C. Qualitative Analysis & Dominant Error Taxonomy

Qualitative examination of generated captions on test scenes reveals three prevalent error modalities: (1) Object Counting Failures: Models struggle to estimate numerical cardinality. In test image #23445819, showing three competitive cyclists racing on a muddy trail, the model predicted "a man is riding a bicycle on a street", substituting plural actors with the ubiquitous singular prior "a man". (2) Background Saliency Over-Attribution: When background features dominate pixel space, models hallucinate actions associated with that background. In image #31298411, a dog leaping mid-air to catch a frisbee above water was captioned as "a dog is swimming in a lake". The massive blue pixel area overpowered the localized kinetic leaping action. (3) Demographic Gender & Profession Bias: Training annotations reflect severe societal priors. In image #41289120, depicting a female construction worker in a safety vest welding steel, the network output "a man in a yellow jacket is working on a road". Industrial tool features overwhelmingly triggered masculine pronouns due to corpus bias.

VI. Hardware & Training Runtime Summary

Experiments executed on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.0 GB VRAM, Google Colab). Feature extraction required 184s (ResNet-50) and 215s (InceptionV3). Model 1 trained in 832s (20 epochs, ~41s/epoch) reaching optimal validation loss (3.0510) at epoch 12. Model 2 trained in 871s, achieving optimal validation loss (2.9415) at epoch 12. Total runtime spanned ~35.0 minutes.

VII. Conclusion

The Merge CNN-LSTM architecture provides an effective baseline for image captioning. While beam search significantly improves BLEU scores, it triggers substantial vocabulary collapse, generating safe, generic sentences. Future work should integrate spatial attention mechanisms (Show, Attend and Tell) and diversity penalties to overcome descriptive homogenization and demographic bias.
