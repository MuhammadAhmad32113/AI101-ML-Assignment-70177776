1. Introduction

Translating between English and Urdu represents a formidable challenge in computational linguistics due to profound structural divergences: English adheres to Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) order and analytic syntax, whereas Urdu is a right-to-left, morphologically rich language following Subject-Object-Verb (SOV) order. In this study, we investigate sequence-to-sequence translation using the OPUS-100 benchmark. We implement a custom bidirectional GRU with Luong attention trained on a curated 75,000-sentence pair subset and compare its performance against the pretrained MarianMT architecture (Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-ur). Our central empirical finding shows that beam search decoding ($k=4$) improves the custom model's sacreBLEU from 18.42 to 20.85 (chrF 41.20), while pretrained MarianMT achieves 29.74 sacreBLEU (chrF 52.18).

3. Dataset Used & Multi-Stage Cleaning

We utilize the OPUS-100 English-Urdu parallel corpus (Hugging Face: Helsinki-NLP/opus-100, config en-ur), consisting of 749,287 raw training pairs. Rigorous cleaning is vital to eliminate noisy subtitles and corrupted alignments. We enforce four sequential filters: (i) deduplication and removal of empty records (−66,877 pairs), (ii) token length ratio $\le 3.0$ (−70,225 pairs), (iii) sequence length $\le 30$ tokens (−143,271 pairs), and (iv) script validation requiring targets to contain valid Arabic/Urdu Unicode blocks (؀–ۿ) (−47,084 pairs). This yields 421,830 clean pairs (56.3% retention). We sample 75,000 clean pairs for training, preserving official 2,000 validation and 2,000 test splits.

5. Methodology

5.1 Tokenization & Model 1 Architecture

To address morphological richness without vocabulary explosion, we train two unigram SentencePiece models with 8,000 subword merges each for English and Urdu. Model 1 implements a 2-layer Bidirectional GRU encoder (embedding dim 256, hidden dim 512, dropout 0.3) paired with a 2-layer unidirectional GRU decoder with Luong multiplicative attention. The model contains 18.4 million trainable parameters. Training uses Adam (lr=$5 imes 10^{-4}$), teacher forcing ratio 0.5, cross-entropy loss ignoring padding, and validation early stopping.

5.2 Model 2 & Decoding Strategies

Model 2 employs the 6-layer encoder-decoder Transformer MarianMT (74M parameters) pretrained on OPUS. For Model 1, we benchmark greedy decoding against beam search ($k=4$) with length penalty $lpha=0.6$. Training was conducted on an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU on Google Colab (22 min 15 sec total time, 10 epochs).

6.1 Gap Analysis & Pretraining Contamination

The 8.89 BLEU gap between Model 1 and MarianMT stems from two factors: (i) model capacity (18M vs. 74M parameters with multi-head self-attention), and (ii) data contamination: MarianMT was pretrained on the entire OPUS corpus (including the full 750k en-ur set and back-translated data), meaning the test set distribution was partially observed during pretraining, whereas Model 1 was restricted to 75,000 clean pairs.

6.3 Translation Quality vs. Sentence Length

As visualized in Fig. 1, BLEU scores decay steadily as sentence length increases: Model 1 scores 24.1 on short sentences (1–5 tokens) but degrades to 14.5 on sentences exceeding 20 tokens. This reflects vanishing gradients and encoder bottlenecking in recurrent architectures, whereas MarianMT maintains 24.1 BLEU even on long sequences due to full cross-attention.

7. Conclusion

Attention-augmented RNNs successfully acquire English-to-Urdu translation (20.85 BLEU), with beam search mitigating greedy compounding errors. Pretrained Transformers remain vastly superior for low-resource pairs. Future work should fine-tune compact multilingual models (e.g. mBART-50) using back-translation.
